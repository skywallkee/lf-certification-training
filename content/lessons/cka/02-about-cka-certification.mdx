## Why the CKA exists

Anyone can read about Kubernetes. What an employer wants to know is whether you can sit down at a cluster you have
never seen, find out why a node is `NotReady`, and bring it back. A multiple-choice test cannot show that: it only
proves you recognise the right answer when it is in front of you.

The **Certified Kubernetes Administrator (CKA)** exam was created by the Cloud Native Computing Foundation (CNCF)
and the Linux Foundation to test the doing. It is a **performance-based** exam: you get a terminal, real clusters
and a list of tasks, and you are scored on the state you leave behind.

<KeyIdea>
The CKA does not ask what you know. It asks what you can get done in two hours at a command line. Study by doing
every topic in a terminal until your hands know it, because there is no time in the exam to work things out for the
first time.
</KeyIdea>

That one fact shapes how this whole course is built: every lesson ends in commands you can run, and the labs and
mock exams matter more than rereading.

## What the certification is

<Diagram id="exam-journey" />

The facts that decide how you plan:

| | |
|---|---|
| **Format** | Online, proctored, performance-based: tasks solved at a command line |
| **Length** | 2 hours for 15 to 20 tasks |
| **Pass mark** | 66% |
| **Kubernetes version** | v1.37 in November 2026; the exam follows each new minor release within about 4 to 8 weeks |
| **Price** | $445 for the exam on its own |
| **Included** | Two exam attempts and two exam simulator sessions (Killer.sh), 36 hours of access each |
| **Time to use it** | 12 months from purchase to schedule and take the exam, second attempt included |
| **Result** | By email within 24 hours of finishing, and in the Linux Foundation portal |
| **Valid for** | 2 years |
| **Prerequisites** | None |
| **Languages** | English, Simplified Chinese and Japanese |

Where it sits among the other certifications:

- The CKA is one of the five exams of the **Kubestronaut** title, with KCNA, KCSA, CKAD and CKS.
- A passed CKA is the **prerequisite for the CKS** (Certified Kubernetes Security Specialist). No other exam requires it.
- The CKAD covers the same platform from the application developer's side. The two overlap in Pods, Deployments,
  Services and configuration, so studying for one is a long way toward the other.

<DeepDive title="Keeping the certification after two years">
- To renew, you take and pass the exam again **before** the certificate expires. The new certificate then runs for
  two more years from the day you pass.
- Passing or renewing the **CKS** on or after 18 June 2026 automatically extends your CKA as well, so if you plan to
  take the CKS anyway, time it to do both jobs.
- A CKA earned before 1 April 2024 was issued for three years rather than two.
- The exam you renew with is the current one. Because the exam tracks new Kubernetes releases, expect topics that
  were not in the curriculum the first time (the Gateway API, Helm and Kustomize are recent additions).
</DeepDive>

## How the exam works

<Diagram id="exam-environment" />

**You sit at home, and a proctor watches.** On your own computer you install one program when the exam starts, the
**PSI Secure Browser**. It shares your camera, microphone and every screen with a remote proctor, and nothing else
may be running. Virtual machines are not accepted, and neither is a second monitor.

**You work on a remote Linux desktop.** The secure browser shows a full Linux desktop with the task list, a terminal
and Firefox. The terminal opens on a machine with the hostname `base`.

**Every task has its own host.** At the top of each task, a box gives you the `ssh` command for the host that task
must be solved on. You connect, do the work, and `exit` back to `base` before the next task. Three rules follow from
that design, and each one costs points when it is forgotten:

- **Tools live on the hosts, not on `base`.** Each host has `kubectl` with the alias `k` and Bash completion, `yq`,
  `curl`, `wget` and man pages. `base` has none of them. If `kubectl` is "not found", you are still on `base`.
- **Nested ssh is not supported.** You cannot hop from one task's host to the next. Go back to `base` first.
- **Never reboot `base`.** It does not restart your exam environment; it only loses you time you cannot get back.

On any host, `sudo -i` makes you root, which you need for work on the node itself: the kubelet, static Pod
manifests, certificates, etcd.

**Scoring is automatic and looks only at the result.** The Linux Foundation states that there may be more than one
way to do a task, and that any path is fine "as long as it produces the correct result". Nobody reads your shell
history. A Deployment created with one imperative command scores exactly like one written by hand in YAML, so the
fastest correct way is always the right way.

<DeepDive title="Why the exam is built this way">
- **A fresh host per task** keeps tasks independent: a mistake in task 3 cannot break task 9. It is also why a
  shell setup you make on one host is gone on the next one, so keep any personal setup to one or two lines.
- **The result is what is graded** because that is what matters in operations. It also means a task that is
  *almost* done can score nothing: a Pod in the wrong namespace, or a file saved under the wrong name, is a
  different result. Read the names in the task twice.
- **The exam follows the newest Kubernetes release** within about 4 to 8 weeks, so study on the current version.
  An API that was removed or a flag that changed in the latest release is fair game from that moment.
- **You get no breakdown.** The score report gives your result, not which tasks you missed, and the Linux Foundation
  does not answer requests for more detail. The simulator and the mock exams in this app are where you find your
  weak spots, because they do show you every check.
</DeepDive>

<Callout kind="warning" title="The timer never pauses">
There is no way to pause the exam or get time back, not even after a lost connection. If you disconnect, you go
through check-in again while the clock runs. Use a wired connection if you can, close everything that could use
bandwidth, and go to the toilet before you start.
</Callout>

## What you are tested on

<Diagram id="domain-weights" />

| Domain | Share | What it covers |
|--------|-------|----------------|
| Troubleshooting | 30% | Clusters and nodes, cluster components, resource usage, container logs, Services and networking |
| Cluster Architecture, Installation and Configuration | 25% | RBAC, kubeadm, cluster lifecycle and upgrades, a highly available control plane, Helm and Kustomize, CNI/CSI/CRI, CRDs and operators |
| Services and Networking | 20% | Pod connectivity, NetworkPolicies, Service types, the Gateway API, Ingress, CoreDNS |
| Workloads and Scheduling | 15% | Deployments and rollouts, ConfigMaps and Secrets, autoscaling, self-healing, scheduling and limits |
| Storage | 10% | StorageClasses and dynamic provisioning, volume types and access modes, PersistentVolumes and claims |

Two things in this table should change how you study:

1. **Troubleshooting is the biggest domain, and it cannot be memorised.** A broken kubelet, a wrong certificate path
   or a Service with no endpoints are found by a routine: look at the status, read the events, read the logs, check
   the config file. You build that routine only by breaking clusters and fixing them, which is what the labs are for.
2. **The percentages are shares of the score, not of the reading.** Storage is 10% of the exam and a small topic;
   those are some of the cheapest points available. Troubleshooting is 30% and draws on everything else.

With a pass mark of 66%, you can lose a third of the points and still pass. The exam is not asking for perfection;
it is asking you not to waste twenty minutes on one task.

## Using it: the two hours in practice

<Example title="The routine for every task">
The same five steps, every time. The host name here is made up; yours is in the box at the top of the task.

```bash
ssh cka-task-07              # 1. the exact command from the task
sudo -i                      # 2. only if the task touches the node itself
hostname && kubectl get nodes   # 3. prove where you are before changing anything
# 4. do the task, then verify the result the way a checker would
exit                         # 5. once for root, once more for ssh, until the prompt says base
```

Step 3 takes three seconds and catches the most expensive mistake in the exam: doing a perfect job on the wrong
cluster. Step 4's "verify" means reading back what you made (`kubectl get`, `kubectl describe`, `cat` the file) and
comparing names and namespaces with the task, character by character.
</Example>

<Example title="A time plan for 120 minutes">
With 15 to 20 tasks you have 6 to 8 minutes per task on average, but tasks are not equal: some take two minutes,
a cluster upgrade or an etcd restore can take fifteen.

| Minutes | What to do |
|---------|------------|
| 0 to 80 | **First pass.** Take the tasks in order. Do everything you know how to do. If a task has made no progress after about 8 minutes, write its number in a text file in the terminal and move on. |
| 80 to 110 | **Second pass.** Return to the tasks on your list, the ones closest to done first. |
| 110 to 120 | **Check.** Re-read the tasks you finished early and confirm names, namespaces and file paths. Do not start anything new. |

Keep your list in a file in the exam terminal. Writing on paper, or on anything outside the exam screen, is against
the rules.
</Example>

<Example title="Finding an answer in the allowed documentation">
You may open these sites in the Firefox browser on the exam desktop, and nothing else:

| Site | Use it for |
|------|-----------|
| `kubernetes.io/docs` | Everything: concepts, tasks, the kubectl and kubeadm reference |
| `kubernetes.io/blog` | Rarely needed |
| `helm.sh/docs` | Helm commands and flags |
| `gateway-api.sigs.k8s.io` | Gateway, HTTPRoute and GatewayClass examples (CKA only) |
| The task's **Quick Reference** box | Links to the documentation of a tool that task needs |

The search box on kubernetes.io is allowed, but you must not open results that lead outside these sites.
Translations are allowed too; the English pages are the most up to date.

The fastest pattern is: search for the object's name, open the **Tasks** or **Concepts** page, copy the smallest
complete manifest, paste it into a file on the host, and edit the names. For example, for a NetworkPolicy search
`network policy`, copy the example from the concepts page, and change the selectors and ports.
</Example>

## Tricks

<Trick title="The documentation is already in your terminal">
`kubectl explain` and `--help` answer most questions faster than a browser, and they always match the cluster's
version:

```bash
kubectl explain pod.spec.containers.livenessProbe      # fields, types and what they mean
kubectl explain deployment.spec.strategy --recursive   # the whole tree at a glance
kubectl create role --help                             # ready-made examples at the top
```
</Trick>

<Trick title="Keep your shell setup to two lines">
Each task's host is a fresh machine, so anything you set up is gone after `exit`. `k` and completion are already
there. If you want more, type only what pays for itself within one task:

```bash
export do="--dry-run=client -o yaml"      # then: k create deploy web --image=nginx $do > web.yaml
k config set-context --current --namespace=shop   # when a task lives in one namespace
```

Practise on a plain shell without your own aliases, so the exam's terminal feels normal.
</Trick>

<Trick title="Learn the desktop's keys before exam day">
In the exam terminal, copy is **Ctrl+Shift+C** and paste is **Ctrl+Shift+V** (or use the right-click menu). In
Firefox it is the usual Ctrl+C and Ctrl+V. **Ctrl+W closes your browser tab**, so to delete a word in the shell use
**Ctrl+Alt+W**. The Insert key is blocked; in `vim`, press `i` to type and `Esc` to stop.
</Trick>

<Trick title="Spend the simulator sessions on purpose">
You get two Killer.sh sessions of 36 hours each. Both sessions contain the same set of questions, so the usual plan
works well: use the first about two weeks before the exam to find your weak topics, and the second a few days
before as a timed rehearsal. Within the 36 hours you can reset and repeat, and the solutions stay available, so
block out the time before you activate one. The simulator is widely found harder than the real exam: a score
near the pass mark there is a good sign.
</Trick>

<Trick title="Paste, do not type">
Copy every name, namespace, image and file path from the task text. A Pod called `web-fronted` instead of
`web-frontend` is a task with zero points, and you will not see why.
</Trick>

## Common mistakes

- **Working on the wrong host.** Forgetting to `exit` and solving the next task on the previous task's cluster. The
  prompt tells you where you are: look at it before the first command of every task.
- **Leaving a task half verified.** The object exists but the Pod is `Pending`, or the file is in your home
  directory instead of the path the task named. Always read the result back.
- **Fighting one task for twenty minutes.** No single task is worth it. The pass mark leaves room to skip the
  hardest ones entirely.
- **Missing the slot.** You can check in from 30 minutes before the booked time. If you have not started within 30
  minutes after it, you are marked as a no-show: the fee is lost and so is the second attempt. Changes to a booking
  are only possible up to 24 hours before it starts.
- **An ID that does not match.** The name on your government-issued photo ID must match the name on your Linux
  Foundation account exactly. Fix the account days before, not at check-in.
- **Breaking a room rule.** Notes on the desk, a second monitor, headphones, reading tasks aloud, leaving the
  camera's view, or a drink that is not a clear liquid in a clear, label-free container can each end the exam.
- **Booking too late.** Slots need at least 24 hours of lead time, and the 12 months cover the second attempt too.
  Leave yourself a few weeks at the end in case you need it.

## Recap

- The CKA is **2 hours of 15 to 20 hands-on tasks**, scored automatically on the result; **66%** passes.
- You work on a remote Linux desktop: **`ssh` to the task's host, do the work, `exit` to `base`**, every time.
- **Troubleshooting (30%)** and **cluster architecture (25%)** are more than half of the score; practise them in labs.
- Only the **Kubernetes, Helm and Gateway API docs** are open, plus `kubectl explain` and `--help`.
- One purchase gives **two attempts and two simulator sessions within 12 months**; the certificate lasts **2 years**.
